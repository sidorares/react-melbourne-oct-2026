# Making it fast

<Lead>Faster code helps. Doing less work helps more.</Lead>

<Grid cols={3} gap={48}>

<Card variant="line" tone="orange" title="Ship the cheapest form">

Commands or pixels, chosen per platform and per drawing

</Card>

<Card variant="line" tone="blue" title="Draw only the newest state">

A UI only has to be eventually consistent

</Card>

<Card variant="line" title="Spend a budget">

High refresh or high detail: you rarely get both

</Card>

</Grid>

<Notes>

Section opener. Everything so far was about correctness: what to repaint and when. This part is about speed, and the honest summary of a year of perf PRs across react-x11 and react-x11-components is that the big wins rarely came from making a function faster.

They came from three kinds of not doing work: shipping the cheapest representation of a drawing for the platform it is going to, never drawing a state that is about to be replaced, and deciding how much each frame is allowed to cost, then degrading detail rather than frame rate when the budget runs out.

Every number in this section is from a merged PR or a design doc in the two repos, with the PR named on the slide.

</Notes>
</Slide>

## Ship drawing commands, or ship pixels?

<Cols template="1fr 1fr" gap={40}>

<Card variant="top" tone="orange" label="X11" title="Send commands; the server draws">

XRender composites, glyph runs, trapezoids. Glyphs and corner shapes are uploaded once and cached on the server.

<Cols template="1fr 1fr" gap={32}>
<Stat value="4.2 kB" label="mount a window of 40 boxes and labels: 110 requests" />
<Stat value="11.2 kB" label="10 wheel notches over 500 rows" />
</Cols>

Works unchanged over SSH.

</Card>

<Card variant="top" tone="blue" label="Cocoa · win32 · Wayland" title="Rasterize here, present pixels">

CoreGraphics into an IOSurface, Direct2D into DirectComposition, a GLES rasterizer of our own on Wayland.

No wire to pay for, but every pixel is CPU or GPU time in our process.

So a pass is cheaper: Cocoa keeps **16** damage rects, X11 keeps 4. Twelve scattered cells: 11.9 → 2.8 ms.

</Card>

</Cols>

<Notes>

The deepest platform difference is where pixels get made. On X11 react-x11 never sends pixels for ordinary UI: it sends drawing commands, and the X server rasterizes them. That is why an app runs over ssh with no changes. The numbers are from the protocol bench baseline in docs/remote.md: mounting a window with 40 boxes and their labels is 110 requests and 4.2 kilobytes on the wire; ten wheel notches through a 500-row list are 11.2 kilobytes.

On Cocoa, win32 and Wayland we rasterize in our own process and hand the system finished pixels, which removes the wire and makes every pixel our CPU's or GPU's problem.

The cost model changes accordingly: a paint pass on X11 is a whole walk of the tree plus server work, so the damage list is capped at four rects; on Cocoa a pass is one CoreGraphics clip, so it keeps sixteen, and twelve scattered cell updates went from 11.9 ms and 1.47 megapixels repainted to 2.8 ms and 0.26 (react-x11#441).

</Notes>
</Slide>

## The rasterization gate: decided per drawing

<Cols template="1fr 560px" gap={48}>
<Col>

For each shape, ntk picks: rasterize the coverage **here** and upload it (PutImage), or send **trapezoids** for the server to fill (AddTraps).

| Policy        | AddTraps | PutImage | bytes out |
| ------------- | -------: | -------: | --------: |
| default gate  |      568 |     2052 |    6.5 MB |
| always local  |      132 |     3119 |   14.1 MB |
| always server |     1211 |        0 |    1.6 MB |

<Small>Six full repaints of a 48-cell wall on XQuartz. Gate defaults: area under 64×64, under 150 bytes per edge.</Small>

</Col>
<Col>

<Card variant="inverse" title="The right answer is per server">

Defaults tuned on XQuartz can cost 10–40× per frame on glamor, where trapezoids are a software fallback.

</Card>

<Card variant="inverse" title="Same lesson, one more time">

Path clips sent as PutImage instead of traps on Xwayland: <O>45–53 ms → 1.2 ms</O> a frame.

</Card>

</Col>
</Cols>

<Notes>

Even on X11 it is not purely commands. For anything that is not a rectangle or a glyph, a rounded shape or a path, ntk has a choice per drawing: compute the coverage mask locally and upload it with PutImage, or describe it as trapezoids and let the server rasterize them. ntk's routeRaster makes that call with two thresholds, the area and the bytes it would cost per edge, and the raster-gate example puts a switch on it.

The table is six full repaints of a wall of 48 cells on XQuartz: always-local more than doubles the bytes on the wire; always-server sends a quarter of the bytes but makes the server rasterize every edge.

The catch is that the best threshold depends on the server: on glamor, the GL-accelerated Xorg backend, trapezoids hit a software fallback that reads back from the GPU, and the XQuartz-tuned defaults can cost ten to forty times more per frame. react-x11#890 is the same lesson for clip paths: switching their coverage to PutImage took rounded marquee rows on Xwayland from 45 to 53 ms a frame down to 1.2.

</Notes>
</Slide>

## Same style, a different bill on every backend

| Style | <O>X11 server</O> | <B>Cocoa · CoreGraphics</B> | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **boxShadow** with blur | a picture filter, re-run on every composite: **1586 ms** for one hover | re-blurred on every fill: **130–380 ms** for a header | blur once, cache the pixels: **1.5 ms** · **4 ms** |
| rounded **overflow** clip | a window-sized mask: **179 ms** server time per pan frame | a roundRect clip: **22 ms** | clip to the rect, keep the corners: **2.4 ms** · zoom 34 → 52 fps |
| a few **animated** nodes | repaint their rects (bounded damage) | repaint the bitmap: **14.58 ms** flush, 108% CPU | promote to CALayers: **0.91 ms**, 54% CPU |

Request counts, bytes and pixels on screen were identical before and after the blur fix. Only a metric for **convolved** pixels saw it.

<Notes>

Same JSX, three very different bills. A blurred box shadow: on X11 it was once left as a convolution filter on the cached picture, which the server re-applies on every composite that reads it, so hovering one card cost 1.6 seconds on XQuartz; baking the blur into the cached pixels made it 1.5 ms. CoreGraphics has the same trap in a different place: it re-blurs a shadow on every fill, and a page header's inset shadow cost 130 to 380 ms until it was drawn from a shared, pre-blurred tile.

Rounded clips: on X11 a non-rectangular clip becomes a window-sized alpha mask per frame; on Cocoa a roundRect clip pushes CoreGraphics off its fast path. The fix in both is to clip to the rectangle and repair the four corners by reading them back.

Animation is where Cocoa can do something X11 cannot: lift the few moving nodes onto their own Core Animation layers so the bitmap under them is not repainted at all.

The quiet lesson at the bottom: the blur regression was invisible to every metric the bench had. That is why it now counts convolved pixels.

</Notes>
</Slide>

## Measure pixels, not requests

<Cols template="1fr 680px" gap={48}>
<Col>

One wheel notch on a 50,000-row table. The only change: how ntk intersects clips.

|                 | ntk 3.10.0 |          3.10.1 |
| --------------- | ---------: | --------------: |
| requests        |       2542 |            1242 |
| Composite calls |        242 |              25 |
| Composite Mpx   |      153.8 | **<O>2.1</O>** |

Each clip had allocated, rasterized and composited a full-window mask.

</Col>
<Col>

<Card title="Requests that cost nothing">

379 clip-rectangle requests per notch, 37% of all traffic. Deleting every one: 681 ms either way.

</Card>

<Card title="Pixels that cost everything">

A blurred boxShadow stored as a picture filter: same requests, same bytes, same pixels, ~700× slower on a real display.

</Card>

</Col>
</Cols>

<Notes>

Two lessons from profiling that apply to any renderer that talks to a server, a GPU or a compositor. First: request count is not cost. ntk brackets every glyph run under a clip with a pair of SetPictureClipRectangles; that was 379 requests a notch and 37 percent of all traffic, and deleting all of them changed frame time by exactly nothing, because they are 8-byte requests with no pixel work behind them.

What did matter was the area the server composites: an old clip intersection that built a full-window a8 mask per nested clip. Fixing it upstream took one notch from 154 megapixels of composite work to 2.

Second: even area is not enough. A blurred box shadow set as a filter on a cached picture is re-convolved by the server on every composite that reads it, so the same request over the same rectangle can cost one tap per pixel or thousands. Every other metric was identical across that fix, which is why the bench now also reports convolved pixels.

</Notes>
</Slide>

## Merge what the next frame would overwrite

<Cols template="1fr 680px" gap={48}>
<Col>

While a frame is pending, events merge instead of queueing:

<table className="mono-first">
  <thead>
    <tr><th>Event</th><th>While a frame is pending</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>mousemove, resize</td><td>keep the last</td></tr>
    <tr><td>expose, damage</td><td>take the union</td></tr>
    <tr><td>wheel</td><td>add the deltas</td></tr>
    <tr><td>press, key</td><td>never merged; flush what's held first</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

The merged ones stay readable in `ev.coalesced`, like the DOM's getCoalescedEvents().

</Col>
<Col>

<Card title="Ask, don't subscribe">

Motion hints plus one QueryPointer per frame. 600 moves: **21.9 → 8.4 KB** from the server, and handlers see as many events (88 vs 84).

</Card>

<Card title="Fewer renders, fresher pixels">

win32, 1 kHz mouse box-selecting 300 nodes: **331 → 36** selection renders, and the position on screen is **11.9 → 4.9 ms** old.

</Card>

</Col>
</Cols>

<Notes>

Section two: the UI only has to be eventually consistent. Between two frames, ten mouse moves are worth exactly one: the last. So ntk keeps a coalescing table. Position-like events keep the newest, region-like events union, wheel deltas add up so no scroll distance is lost, and discrete events (presses, keys) are never merged and flush anything held before them, so ordering is preserved where it matters.

The older events are not destroyed: a drawing app can read the full path from ev.coalesced, like getCoalescedEvents on the web.

Two measurements. On X11, asking for motion with PointerMotionHint and polling once per frame cut the bytes from the server by 60% while delivering as many events to handlers. On win32, holding motion until the next frame turned 331 selection renders of a box-select on a 1 kHz mouse into 36, and the counter-intuitive part: the pointer position on screen got fresher, 11.9 ms old down to 4.9, because the frames stopped queueing behind stale renders.

</Notes>
</Slide>

## Slow display: fewer frames, never a queue

<Cols template="1fr 600px" gap={48}>
<Col>

After each frame, ntk sends a **fence**: one round trip. The next frame waits for its reply, so the server's speed becomes our frame rate.

Where the server has Present, the clock is the display itself. A window nobody can see drops to about 1 fps instead of 56 (Mutter).

| Network round trip | 1 frame in flight |         2 in flight |
| ------------------ | ----------------: | ------------------: |
| 10 ms              |         69–71 fps | <B>99–100 fps</B> |
| 40 ms              |         22–23 fps |  <B>44–47 fps</B> |

</Col>
<Col>

<Card title="First input now, the rest folded">

Ten wheel notches: the first paints immediately, the other nine fold into one catch-up frame.

</Card>

<Card title="A burst of discrete events">

~450 events in a second: <O>449 → 61</O> blits, with the first still out in under 1 ms.

</Card>

</Col>
</Cols>

<Notes>

The second half of eventual consistency is backpressure. If a renderer produces frames faster than the display consumes them, they queue, and the user watches a replay of the past. ntk ends every frame with a fence, a cheap request whose reply proves the server has processed everything before it, and the next frame waits for that reply. On a slow server or a slow network you get fewer, bigger frames that are always current.

Where the server supports the Present extension the clock is the display's own completion events, which also means a window nobody can see stops drawing.

One fence in flight halves the frame rate on a laggy link, so ntk#370 allows two: through a proxy adding 10 ms, 70 to 100 fps; at 40 ms, 22 to 45.

Discrete input gets special treatment on top: the first wheel notch paints from its own handler, and the following ones fold into one catch-up frame; a flood of 450 key events in a second goes out as 61 blits.

</Notes>
</Slide>

<Notes>

The same principle one level up, in application and component code. The terminal emulator used to subscribe to every scrolled line, which was 336,000 repaint claims for a 300,000-line cat; one subscription per parsed batch is under 600, and a flood went from 3.5 s to about 1 s.

A Frame pane, a child process embedded in our window, used to receive every rect of a window drag in order, so its final size arrived up to 2.5 seconds after the mouse stopped; now one rect is in flight at a time and it is always the newest, so the pane gets 31 instead of 121 and catches up in 39 ms. A fast fling through a 100,000-row table mounted 1,250 rows it showed for a single frame; trimming to the rows the frame can show took it from 16 to 39 fps.

An animation nobody can see stops. The accessibility bridge pushes a stream at most twice a second, the way Chromium batches its accessibility events, and a row that scrolls in and out between pushes is simply never exported.

And React's own tool for this is useDeferredValue, used in the monitor example so the filter input stays responsive while a large list catches up.

</Notes>

## Price frames in CPU time: a token bucket

**The bug:** a `cat` flood took 3.50 s on Cocoa, 0.85 s on XQuartz. X11's fence held it to 35 fps. Cocoa had no backpressure and painted 110 fps, 60% of the time.

<Cols template="1fr 820px" gap={48}>
<Col gap={16}>

<O>■</O> Credit accrues at **budget × wall time** (adaptive: a quarter), up to a 50 ms burst.

<O>■</O> A frame spends what its flush and present cost. In debt, the next waits.

<O>■</O> A click or key is never held. A cheap frame never waits: a blitted scroll stays at 120 Hz.

<O>■</O> Server time is never priced: the fence already paces it.

</Col>
<Col gap={12}>

<table className="mono-first">
  <thead>
    <tr><th>frameRate</th><th align="right">fps</th><th align="right">paint</th><th align="right">CPU</th><th align="right">producer</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>'display'</td><td align="right">120</td><td align="right">58%</td><td align="right">72%</td><td align="right">244/s</td></tr>
    <tr className="hl-orange"><td>'adaptive'</td><td align="right">36.1</td><td align="right">21%</td><td align="right">27%</td><td align="right"><b>529/s</b></td></tr>
    <tr><td>'throughput'</td><td align="right">8.8</td><td align="right">9%</td><td align="right">13%</td><td align="right">592/s</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

<Small>A window repainting whole on a 2 ms timer. M1 Pro, 120 Hz. Painting every refresh starves the producer of half its ticks.</Small>

</Col>
</Cols>

<Notes>

Section three: budgets. The motivating bug was a terminal: cat a large file and Cocoa took four times longer than XQuartz on the same Mac. On X11 the fence was quietly pacing the app to about 35 fps because the server needs time to composite a screen of glyphs. Cocoa had nothing equivalent, so it painted every refresh and spent 60% of its time painting instead of parsing.

The fix, react-x11#497, is a token bucket priced in CPU time. A window earns credit at a fraction of wall time (a quarter for adaptive), each frame spends what its flush and present actually cost, and a claim made while in debt waits. Three rules keep it from making the app feel slower: discrete input is never held, cheap frames never wait, so a blitted scroll still runs at 120 Hz, and the X server's own time is never charged, because the fence already accounts for it.

The table is a synthetic stream that repaints the whole window on a 2 ms timer: at the default it paints 120 frames a second and the producer gets half its ticks; adaptive paints 36 frames and the producer gets all of them, at about a third of the CPU.

It is opt-in, per window, and it was the default-plus-seam rule in action: 'display' by default, a prop to change it.

</Notes>

Browsers make the same trade: low-resolution tiles and checkerboarding on a fast scroll, cheaper paint during a window resize.

<Notes>

The other half of a budget is what you do when you are over it. The pattern that recurs across the components is the one browsers use: while the user's hand is moving, frame rate matters more than detail, and once it stops, detail matters more than frame rate, and nobody notices the switch if the settled frame is exact.

Flow's GL renderer zooms by scaling the scene it already has and rebuilds 120 ms after the wheel stops; labels are signed distance fields, so a zoom does no text work at all, and gesture frames went from about 110 to about 140 fps. The Html component watches its own frame cost during a live resize and, when it falls behind, snaps scaled backgrounds to a coarse grid and pauses animations, then repaints exactly once the drag rests.

Core does the same with layout: the expensive content-floor measurement waits for the end of a window resize, and the 80 frames that used to queue up behind a drag became one. And tables show skeleton rows on a scrubbed scrollbar and fill them viewport-first. The browser parallels are analogies, not code shared with any browser.

</Notes>

## Adaptive quality: the map's ladder

<Cols template="1fr 600px" gap={56}>
<Col gap={12}>

<Eyebrow>A moving frame gets 12 ms. Drop, in order:</Eyebrow>

<QualityLadder />

<Small>Still for 140 ms: rung 0, sharp.</Small>

</Col>
<Col gap={22}>

**Predict, then draw.** Each frame's cost is estimated from what it will draw, before it is drawn.

**Climb back slowly.** A richer rung only once it is predicted under 75% of the budget, so it doesn't flicker.

<Card variant="inverse" tone="orange" title="Don't stall the frame to measure it">

Timing moving frames with glFinish read 16–52 ms for a few ms of drawing, and sent nearly every frame to the coarsest rung. It now learns from settled frames only.

</Card>

</Col>
</Cols>

<Notes>

The clearest example of trading detail for frame rate is the GL map in react-x11-components. A moving frame has a 12 ms budget. Before drawing, the renderer predicts the frame's cost by walking the same gates the frame will walk over the record counts in the visible tiles, times a learned milliseconds-per-instance. It then picks the richest rung of a ladder that fits.

The rungs drop what the picture misses least first: the half-pixel antialiasing pass on polygon edges, which is a fifth of a dense frame and invisible in motion; then the style's newest detail layers, which the style itself marks as details by bringing them in last; then tiles from a coarser zoom level, whose generalized geometry is cheaper. It climbs back only when the richer rung is predicted under 75% of the budget, so a frame sitting at the limit does not flicker between two levels. When the camera has been still for 140 ms it draws at full quality.

The lesson from the PRD is worth stealing: the first version timed every moving frame with glFinish, which stalls the thread until the GPU is done, so it measured 16 to 52 ms for frames that cost a few, concluded the machine was slow, and drew nearly everything at the bottom rung. A mechanism whose job is frame rate must not stall the frame to observe itself.

Measured with the budget forced down to 3 ms: panning London stayed on rungs 1 and 2 for 305 of 363 frames.

</Notes>
</Slide>

## A frame-rate plateau is a scheduler bug

<Cols template="1fr 600px" gap={48}>
<Col gap={20}>

{'<glarea>'} on a 120 Hz panel, by synthetic draw cost:

| draw cost |   before |               after |
| --------- | -------: | ------------------: |
| ~0 ms     | 67.2 fps | <B>119.7 fps</B> |
| 3 ms      | 59.8 fps | <B>119.6 fps</B> |
| 10 ms     | 52.0 fps |  <B>92.4 fps</B> |

Two gates, each one period long, in series: a frame landed two periods later. On a 75 Hz monitor, a frame timer between 8 ms pump ticks took 56.3 → 70.4 fps.

</Col>
<Card tone="orange" title="The rules">

**Round a frame wait up.** 120 Hz is 8.333 ms; a timer of 8 fires before the gate it was armed for.

**Anchor to the last slot**, not to when the frame ran, so rounding never drifts.

**One grid.** An extra hold asks the clock for its next slot instead of starting its own timer.

**The tell:** two workloads, one frame rate. A p50 of exactly two periods is not a cost.

</Card>
</Cols>

<Notes>

The last lesson is about measuring frame rate at all. When two quite different workloads report the same frame rate, the work is not what limits you; the scheduler is. A glarea on a 120 Hz MacBook ran at 60 to 67 fps whether a frame cost nothing or 3 ms. The cause was two gates in series, a swap gate armed with a timer for one period after the swap and the window's frame clock, each honest on its own, and together landing every frame on the second refresh. Phase-locking the swap gate to the window clock's next slot took it to 120 fps, and the 10 ms row shows what a real cost curve looks like afterwards.

The same family of bug turned a 75 Hz monitor into 62.5 or 56 fps through an 8 ms pump. The rules that came out of three of these: always round a frame wait up, because a refresh period is a fraction and setTimeout counts whole milliseconds; anchor the next slot to the previous slot so rounding does not accumulate; and when something must hold a frame back on top of a clock, express it on that clock's grid.

And before reading a profile, compare the median frame gap with the display period: exactly two periods means a scheduling bug. One more from the map PRD: a run that came in at 60 instead of 75 fps looked like a cost, until a control run showed the display had changed its rate.

</Notes>
</Slide>
